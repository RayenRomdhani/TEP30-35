Flip a light switch in Tunis and, about 19 times out of 20, the power comes from burning natural gas. That is not a figure of speech: in 2024, **94.5%** of the electricity Tunisia generated came from gas. Solar, wind, hydro and a little oil made the rest.

## Electricity in Tunisia, 2024

| | 2024 | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Electricity used in the country | 22.5 TWh | ONEM Memento 2024 |
| ...of which generated in Tunisia | 19.2 TWh | ONEM |
| ...of which imported (mainly from Algeria) | 3.1 TWh | ONEM |
| Share of generation from gas | 94.5% | ONEM |
| Highest demand in one moment (the peak) | 4,888 MW, on 14 August at 15:41 | ONEM, STEG |
| Power plants installed | 6,012 MW, of which 5,644 MW are thermal (gas-fired) plants | ONEM |

All of these are rows in the [assumptions register](../../data/assumptions_register.md), each with its source link.

One company sits at the centre of all this: **STEG**, the state-owned electricity and gas company, founded in 1962. It runs the high-voltage grid and the distribution networks, supplies natural gas, and produced about 86% of the country's electricity in the years covered by the JICA survey (2022). Private producers have been allowed to build plants since 1996.

## Where the gas comes from

Here is the twist that makes the topic interesting. In 2024 Tunisia's natural gas came from three places (ONEM, in thousand tonnes of oil equivalent):

| Source | 2024 (ktep) | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Tunisian gas fields | 1,212 | 27% |
| Royalty on the Algerian gas that crosses Tunisia to Italy, paid in gas | 909 | 21% |
| Gas bought from Algeria | 2,290 | 52% |

So **about three quarters of the gas comes from Algeria**, one way or another, and domestic production fell by a quarter in a single year (from 1,607 to 1,212 ktep). Power plants burned 3,168 ktep of it, which is **about 72% of the country's whole gas supply**. Across all forms of energy, Tunisia's own resources covered only 41% of what it used in 2024 (ONEM's "energy independence rate").

Put simply: every MWh of electricity Tunisia uses is, mostly, gas that comes from a neighbour.

## The 2030 promise

Tunisia has been trying to change this for a while:

- **2012:** the Tunisian Solar Plan aims for 30% renewable electricity by 2030.
- **2015:** the country's climate pledge (INDC) targets a 41% cut in CO2 emission intensity by 2030, compared with 2010.
- **2022:** the renewable target is raised to **35% by 2030**, and reaffirmed by the Head of Government in October 2026.

Going from about 5% to 35% renewable electricity in a few years is a big jump. And demand keeps growing: STEG's forecast is **3.2% more electricity every year**. Every extra MWh of demand is another MWh that needs either more renewables or more gas.

## So what can efficiency do?

There are two ways to close a gap: build more supply, or need less of it. This study looks at the second one:

- **Energy efficiency:** the same comfort and output with less electricity (better air conditioners, insulation, efficient motors, LED lights).
- **Demand flexibility:** using electricity at a different time, for example running a water heater at noon when the sun is shining instead of at 7 pm.

The question I asked:

> **If Tunisia uses one MWh less in 2030, what does its power system no longer have to buy, burn or build?**

Answering that needs a model of the whole power system, because one MWh saved at 3 pm in August is not worth the same as one saved at 3 am in April. That is what the next chapters build up to.
